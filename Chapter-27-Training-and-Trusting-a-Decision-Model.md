# Chapter 27 — Training and Trusting a Decision Model: Data, Calibration and Evaluation

## Introduction

Chapter 26 took the architecture apart: an encoder with a `[MASK]` slot for each option (Laya) and a decoder with a readout at the end of each option (Vega). An architecture alone gives random numbers. This chapter is about what turns it into a decision model people can rely on: the **data** it learns from, the **training** that teaches it to give good probabilities, the **cost** of that training, the **calibration** that keeps its confidence honest, and the **evaluation** that tells you whether any of it worked.

Laya is again the main worked example, because its code, documentation and benchmark files are public. Vega and Jev appear where their makers say something about their own data or training.

The parts of this chapter:

1. **Data:** where labelled decisions come from.
2. **Training:** how the model learns to give good probabilities, including the "reinforcement learning" in the name.
3. **What training costs:** and why fine-tuning is so much cheaper than pretraining.
4. **Calibration:** making the probabilities honest.
5. **Keeping calibration alive:** updating it as the world drifts.
6. **Evaluation:** testing honestly.
7. **Limits and open questions.**

> **Sources and numbers.** As in the previous chapter, facts about Laya come from its repository, model cards and documentation, facts about Vega from its model card and code, and the authors' own claims are labelled as such. Small worked examples use illustrative numbers, and some were produced by the short programs in `Chapters-26-27-experiments.js` so that you can rerun them (`node Chapters-26-27-experiments.js all`).

## Part 1 — Data: where labelled decisions come from

**The problem.** To train the model of Chapter 26, Part 2 you need many examples of **a state, a question with its allowed answers, and the correct answer**. Where do thousands of those come from, and what *form* should "the correct answer" take?

### 1.1 The sources, from most human to least

| Source | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human labels** | people answer each question | trustworthy | slow, costly, people disagree |
| **Existing labelled datasets, recast as decisions** | an old classification dataset becomes `choice`, `score` or `noul` questions | cheap, real text | the labels were designed for something else |
| **A teacher model** | one or more strong LLMs answer each decision, and their answers (averaged) are the label | scalable, gives probabilities | the student cannot beat the teacher's blind spots |
| **Synthetic states** | an LLM also *writes* the example tickets and emails | unlimited volume | may not look like real traffic |
| **Real logs with outcomes** | production decisions plus what happened next | realistic | needs the outcome to be observed |

### 1.2 What the systems say they do

**Laya** (documentation and repository):

- Its training mix includes **public labelled datasets recast as decisions**. The benchmark files name AG News, BoolQ, email spam, phishing, RAG passage relevance and a support-triage set as "in training". Each dataset's label set simply becomes the `criteria` text. Training on many datasets with many different label sets is what teaches a model to *read* the options and not memorise a label list. (Related earlier work: "label-agnostic pre-training" for zero-shot text classification.) The author's own account describes the base mix as "100% human-labeled" public datasets with no synthetic labels, augmented by shuffling option order, paraphrasing, alternating raw text and JSON, and adding distractor questions. No dataset names are given, so this is a claim.
- Its **fine-tuning loop learns from a teacher's probabilities**, which its documentation calls `gold`: the probability the teacher gave each option.
- Its fine-tuned checkpoint was trained on the public `typed-decisions` dataset (Part 1.4): 1,200 training cases (6,000 decisions), tested on 400 cases (2,000 decisions) across four workflows: agent-trace observability, customer service, invoice processing and security incidents.

**Jev** (TypeSafe's launch post and press): the launch post's FAQ heading "Where does our training data come from?" has no answer under it. Wikipedia and TechCrunch report training on synthetic data only. For its *evaluations*, the reference answers are the average of two large models.

**Decision 2.0 / Vega:** the model card says only that the training data was checked row by row against all the Index test items, a contamination check. A write-up from the same team about a *different* family (Vela, built on Decision 2.0) describes label resampling (options are anonymised, dropped and paraphrased so the model learns to read labels), synthetic rows generated by a Qwen3-30B model and kept only when an independent re-label agreed (71% did), and deduplication against evaluation sets. That is Vela's recipe, not Vega's.

### 1.3 The form of the label: a distribution, not one answer

Laya's training items come in two shapes:

- `expected`: one hard answer (a choice label, `true`/`false` for a noul, a level for a score; a fractional level like 1.5 splits its weight between levels 1 and 2). A label-smoothing option moves a tenth of each answer's weight to the other options.
- `gold`: the teacher's **probability for every option**, such as `{"billing": 0.9, "technical": 0.1}`.

**Why a distribution matters.** Suppose two teacher models disagree on a ticket: one says billing, the other says other. The soft target is `[billing 0.5, technical 0, other 0.5]`. The loss is cross-entropy (Chapter 9), computed for three candidate model outputs:

```text
model output                          loss vs the soft target    loss vs a hard label "billing"
overconfident [0.98, 0.01, 0.01]              2.31                       0.02   (looks great!)
matches teachers [0.495, 0.01, 0.495]         0.70                       0.70
wrong side [0.02, 0.01, 0.97]                 1.97                       3.91
```

Trained on the hard label, the overconfident model looks nearly perfect. Trained on the soft target, it is punished heavily, because the teachers were *not sure*. A soft label teaches the model **how sure to be**. That is where calibration starts (Part 4).

### 1.4 The `typed-decisions` dataset

`LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions` on Hugging Face is the dataset Laya's fine-tuning notebook loads. Its card says:

- Apache-2.0 licence, tagged synthetic, and **independent of TypeSafe** (the card says it is not affiliated with the company and does not reproduce Jev). The schema follows the System 1 kinds of question.
- Each case has five typed questions about one piece of state; the test split is 400 cases and 2,000 decisions.
- **Gold is the mean of three samples from a teacher of roughly 4B-class capability** (not named). So a score "measures agreement with that teacher, not correctness". The card gives a missed duplicate invoice as an example of the teacher being wrong.
- Reference points on its test: always answering the common label scores 0.470, a model that perfectly recovered the hidden factors that generated each case scores 0.704, and the **teacher agreeing with itself** (a fresh sample against gold built from the others) scores **0.735**. The card warns that scores well above 0.735 suggest a model is learning the teacher's quirks. Per-question ceilings range from 0.560 (agent-trace urgency) to 0.937 (customer-service category).

### 1.5 Open datasets on Hugging Face

Laya's authors have not published a dataset repository of their own. Others are open:

| Dataset | What it is | Licence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions` | the benchmark Laya fine-tunes on | Apache-2.0 | synthetic; gold from a ~4B teacher |
| `typesafe/evalsafe-*` (5 sets) | TypeSafe's own evaluation sets (customer service is 204 cases, 3,287 question instances) | not shown in the metadata | "labels are model-generated references" |
| `ZefanCai/Open-Jev` | typed-decision training data, up to about 160k rows | CC0 | mostly synthetic; says it is not official TypeSafe data |
| `tasksource/tasksource-jev-typed-decisions` | 2.5 million typed decisions from 670 sources | "other" (read it) | recast public datasets |
| `tasksource/procedural-typed-decisions` | procedurally generated problems | Apache-2.0 | |
| `pngwn/system-one-decisions` | synthetic IT tickets | **CC BY-NC 4.0** | non-commercial |

Check licences before releasing a fine-tune: the data's licence matters, and one of these is non-commercial.

### 1.6 Seen versus unseen data

Laya's benchmark file marks each dataset as "in training" or "held out" (not in the training mix):

| Task | Accuracy | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Email spam | 0.993 | in training |
| Phishing | 0.993 (multilingual) | in training |
| Jailbreak guardrails | 0.708 to 0.762 | **held out** |
| Moderation (toxicity) | 0.530 | **held out** |

Only the held-out rows tell you how the model handles new data. The authors say so plainly: moderation is barely above chance on a balanced split, and examples picked by hand work while real traffic does not. The same pattern shows in the author's own summary: 83.8% accuracy in-task (on data in the training mix) against 65.1% zero-shot.

### 1.7 How much data, and what it buys

On the typed-decisions test, Laya's base checkpoint scores **0.362** and the fine-tuned one **0.766** (the authors' figures; the README notes that the fine-tuned row has no committed result file behind it yet). That used 6,000 decisions and took about 4 to 6 minutes on two T4 GPUs (Part 3). Small, well-aimed data makes a very large difference.

**A typical data plan.** Write the questions and criteria (Chapter 25). Collect real states from the system the model will serve. Get labels from people, a teacher, or several teachers averaged into probabilities. **Hold out two slices before training**: a small slice to fit calibration (Laya uses up to 400 items or 10%), and a test set to judge the result. Fitting the temperature on training items "measures the fit rather than the calibration" (Laya's docs).

## Part 2 — Training: how the model learns to give good probabilities

This part is told as one idea in six steps, with a single small example. (Laya's documentation calls its training "reinforcement learning", and the last steps explain what that really means.)

### 2.1 The idea: a student and an answer key

Imagine a student studying with an **answer key**, but a special key: instead of "the answer is B", it says "70% of experts say B, 20% say A, 10% say C". The student reads a question, gives percentages, compares them with the key, and adjusts to get closer. After thousands of questions, the student's percentages match what the experts would say. That is the whole objective: **the student** is the network, **the answer key** is the teacher's probabilities from Part 1, the comparison is a score called the loss, and "adjusting" is the nudge.

### 2.2 The dials

For each option the network ends up with one number, a "dial" setting (the technical name is a *logit*). A softmax turns the dials into percentages. A higher dial means a higher percentage.

```text
question + options → [the whole network] → one dial per option → softmax → percentages
                                                                     ↓
                                                        compare with the teacher's key
```

### 2.3 One worked example

Three options. The teacher's key says **70% / 20% / 10%**. The network's dials are `[0.50, 0.40, −0.20]`, which gives **42% / 38% / 21%**. Far from the key. The loss, cross-entropy (Chapter 9), asks "how surprised would the key be by the student's answer?" and here it is **0.966**.

The **nudge** is *key minus student*, option by option: `70−42, 20−38, 10−21` → `+0.28, −0.18, −0.11`. It says: turn the first dial up, the other two down. Following it repeatedly:

```text
step 0: percentages 42/38/21   loss 0.966   nudge [+0.28, −0.18, −0.11]
step 1:             63/23/14   loss 0.815   nudge [+0.07, −0.03, −0.04]
step 2:             68/20/12   loss 0.804
step 4:             70/19/11   loss 0.802   nudge ≈ 0
step 8:             70/20/10   loss 0.802   done
```

Three things to notice:

1. **The nudge shrinks by itself** as the student gets closer, which is why training settles down.
2. **The loss never reaches zero.** It stops at **0.802**, the teacher's own uncertainty. A key that says "70/20/10" cannot be matched with zero surprise.
3. With a **single hard label** `[1, 0, 0]` from the same start, the student ends at **96% / 2% / 2%**, far too sure. The soft key teaches the student *how unsure to be*.

### 2.4 How the nudge reaches the transformer

In real training the dials are not turned directly. They come out of the network (the scorer, the two head layers, the 28 encoder layers). So the nudge `+0.28, −0.18, −0.11` is handed **backwards** through the network (backpropagation, Chapter 10; Chapter 26, Part 1.6). Every layer gets a small push so that next time the dials land a bit closer to the key. Each question gives one small push, and thousands of them make a trained model. The transformer's shape never changes, and only its numbers move.

```text
tokens → ModernBERT encoder (28 layers) → +type vector → 2 head layers → [MASK] slot vectors → scorer
                                                                                       │
                                                                          logits z (one per option)
                                                                                       │
                          ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
                          ▼                                                            ▼
              soft cross-entropy                                      RL term (noise → reward → advantage)
              gradient on z:  p − t                                   gradient on z:  advantage × noise / σ²
                          └──────────────── either way a vector on z ──────────────────┘
                                            flows back through everything
```

### 2.5 Ordered levels (`score` questions)

For ordered levels, cross-entropy only looks at how much probability sits on the *correct* level. Chapter 25, Part 3 showed two answers that both give the right level 0.10, one putting the rest of its weight one level away and the other two levels away: cross-entropy scores both 2.30, while the **ranked probability score** gives 0.90 and 1.17. Laya adds this term for `score` questions only. It is a direct, differentiable score, so no reinforcement learning is involved.

### 2.6 What the "reinforcement learning" part adds

In plain terms it is a coaching method: **try small random variations of your answer, see which scored better, and move toward those**. Laya's loss also contains a cross-entropy term at full weight. The RL step, in Laya's code, for each training question:

1. **Explore.** Add Gaussian noise to the dials (noise size σ = 0.4 early, 0.1 late), four times. Each noisy version becomes a "reported" distribution.
2. **Reward each report** with a proper scoring rule: log score + 0.75 × spherical score, minus the ranked probability score on `score` questions. The log score is exactly negative cross-entropy.
3. **Advantage** = reward minus the average reward of the group of four, then scaled. This is the group-relative idea of GRPO from Chapter 20, where the "group" is the four noisy attempts at the same input.
4. **Gradient** on the dials = advantage × noise ÷ σ². Noise that helped is reinforced; noise that hurt is reversed.
5. **Add soft cross-entropy** at full weight.

(A detail from the community: the code builds the noisy dials from a *detached* copy of the logits but computes the log-probability from the live ones. A user who ported it without the detach found the RL term silently vanished.)

With the same example (key 70/20/10, student 42/38/21):

| Try | Resulting answer | Score | Better or worse than the average try? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 71 / 16 / 13 | −0.26 | better |
| 2 | 30 / 47 / 23 | −0.73 | **worse** |
| 3 | 42 / 25 / 32 | −0.51 | slightly worse |
| 4 | 59 / 31 / 10 | −0.30 | better |

Moving toward the tries that scored better and away from those that scored worse nudges the dials the right way, **about the same way as the plain nudge**. In a simulation of this exact example, the update direction of the plain cross-entropy nudge and the exact reward gradient were nearly identical (cosine 0.999). The four-try RL estimate agreed with the exact gradient on average (cosine 0.83, spread 0.20), reaching 0.99 with 64 tries, and its size grows about fourfold when σ drops from 0.4 to 0.1 because of the 1/σ² factor.

Because the score and the key are both known, the best direction can be **calculated directly**: that is cross-entropy's nudge. The random tries are a noisy route to the same place. A third-party A/B test of Laya's own fine-tuning notebook, on its own data and settings (single run, one seed, issue #741), compared the options on about 1,200 test decisions:

```text
plain soft cross-entropy only             80.50%
Laya's RL + cross-entropy (notebook)      79.08%
the reward computed directly (no noise)   79.92%
```

On roughly 1,200 questions those gaps are inside the noise (Part 6). The evidence says the RL step is not helping, and Laya's documentation itself offers a plain cross-entropy mode.

**When would RL matter?** When the score cannot be computed directly: when the cost of acting wrongly versus escalating is a trade-off, or when the true outcome only shows up later (a sale closing weeks afterwards). Laya's helper code contains pieces for both (action costs for the act/escalate head, and a TD(λ) function that credits earlier turns of a conversation with the final outcome), but the public training loop does not use them. Its forward helper throws the act head's output away.

### 2.7 What a real training run looks like

1. Shuffle the training examples and take a small batch of 8.
2. Run the model on them, get the dials, compute the loss against the teacher keys.
3. Nudge everything backwards. After 64 examples have been seen (the "effective batch of 64"), update the weights.
4. Make four passes over the data (four epochs).
5. Use **two learning rates**: gentle (2.5e-5) for the already-trained encoder and bigger (1e-4) for the brand-new head, like asking an experienced reader to adjust slightly while a new assistant learns quickly. The optimiser is AdamW (Chapter 11) with a cosine schedule and gradient clipping at 1.0.
6. Fit the temperature on held-out examples (Part 4), then test on separate data (Part 6).

### 2.8 How it evolved

| When | What |
|---|---|
| 2025 | Reinforcement learning on top of frozen embeddings (the sales model, Chapter 26, Part 2.1) |
| At release, by the author's account | Pure RL training an end-to-end encoder: REINFORCE with a group-mean baseline over 8 noisy samples (noise 1.0 down to 0.3), reward = log score + 0.5 × spherical − ranked probability score, **no cross-entropy**, plus TD(λ) for multi-turn data and action costs (+1 correct, −3 wrong, −0.5 escalate) |
| 19 to 20 September 2026 | Users ask for the original training scripts and how to train |
| 29 September | The independent A/B test above: no measurable gain from the RL term |
| 2 to 7 October | A `laya.train` module merges three drifting copies of the training loop, with CSV loading, a plain `soft-ce` mode, option-order shuffling and abstention-threshold fitting |
| Now | Notebook default: 4 samples, noise 0.4 to 0.1, spherical weight 0.75, **plus full-weight cross-entropy** |

The trend is clear: pure RL on frozen features, then RL plus supervised learning end to end, then community evidence that supervised learning alone is enough. Even the reward function's own defaults (spherical weight 0.5) still carry the original value. (The "original recipe" comes from the author's account, and the repository history available covers only the period from September 2026.)

**Vega** uses the same family of ideas without RL: a cross-entropy loss with an optional Brier term and a KL term that keeps the model from forgetting (Chapter 26, Part 3.5).

### 2.9 Which loss, when

The default is **soft cross-entropy on teacher distributions**. For `score` questions, add the ranked-probability term as an ordinary differentiable loss. Reach for RL only when the reward is genuinely non-differentiable or delayed, such as action costs or outcomes observed later.

## Part 3 — What training costs

**Is fine-tuning still like training a GPT?** No. It is the cheapest tier of training there is, closer to teaching a trained employee a new form than to raising and educating one from scratch.

### 3.1 Three scales side by side

Compute is estimated with the standard rule of about 6 × parameters × tokens. The Laya row rests on an assumed ~250 tokens per training item, so treat it as an estimate.

| What | Data processed | Approx. compute | Hardware and time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BERT-base pretraining** | 1,000,000 steps × 128,000 tokens ≈ 128 billion tokens | ~6.5 × 10¹⁹ FLOP | 16 TPU chips (from the paper) |
| **ModernBERT-large pretraining** | 2 trillion tokens | ~4 × 10²¹ FLOP | a large GPU cluster |
| **Laya fine-tune** (6,000 decisions, 4 epochs) | ~6 million tokens (assumed) | ~1.3 × 10¹⁶ FLOP | **about 4 to 6 minutes on two T4 GPUs** (from Laya's docs) |

A Laya fine-tune is roughly **5,000 times** less compute than pretraining BERT-base and about **300,000 times** less than pretraining ModernBERT-large.

**Why it is so cheap.** It starts from a pretrained model (Chapter 26, Part 1.6); it uses little data; it makes few passes (4 epochs); inputs are short; and the model is small. Training needs about 16 bytes per parameter in mixed precision (a rule of thumb), so 421M parameters is roughly **7 GB** before activations, which fits a 16 GB card. That is what Laya's notebook targets (it runs on Kaggle's free two-T4 tier).

**Where cost can still hide.**

- **Labels.** Getting good answers for a few thousand examples is often a bigger job than the GPU time.
- **Experiments multiply it.** You will run it more than once (tuning, comparing, recalibrating).
- **Longer inputs cost more.** Cost scales with *tokens*, not examples. Laya's docs give about 4 to 6 minutes for 6,000 decisions but roughly 4 to 5 hours for about 30,000 questions: 5× the data, about 50× the time, which suggests the larger run has much longer inputs (an inference, since the docs do not say).
- **A bigger backbone costs far more** (below).

**It is simple to run.** Laya's documentation shows the whole thing as one command over a CSV with a text column and a label column:

```bash
laya-train --data tickets.csv --text-column body --label-column department --base english --out ./tickets-checkpoint
```

There is a `--dry-run` to check your data first, and it runs on CUDA, Apple Silicon (MPS) or CPU. A `--freeze-encoder` option trains only the head and fits a small GPU or CPU, but the docs say it "gains much less" than a full fine-tune.

### 3.2 What LoRA saves, and what it does not (Vega)

Vega is a LoRA adapter on a full Qwen base, so it is tempting to assume its training is nearly free. It is cheaper than pretraining Qwen, which Vega's team inherits. But:

- LoRA freezes the base and trains small add-on matrices. That reduces **how many numbers get updated**, not **how many get computed**. Every step still runs forward through all 64 layers, and the backward pass still carries gradients through the frozen layers to reach the adapters. It skips only the weight-gradient work for frozen weights, so the cost per token drops from about 6 × parameters to about **4 × parameters** (and back toward 6 × with activation checkpointing). The real saving is **memory**: no gradients or Adam statistics for 27B weights.
- Vega's adapter is **not small** (3.74 billion parameters, 13% of the base), and it is a soup of at least four runs (Chapter 26, Part 3.5).

| | Laya full fine-tune | LoRA on the 27B (one rank-128 member) |
|---|---|---|
| Compute per token | ~2.2 GFLOP | **~103 GFLOP** (about 152 with checkpointing), **46 to 68×** more |
| Training memory | ~7 GB | ~51 GB frozen base + ~15 GB for the adapter's training state ≈ **66 GB**, before activations |
| Hardware | one or two 16 GB GPUs | an 80 GB+ class GPU |
| Runs | one, maybe a few seeds | at least four (the soup) |

For an illustration of time at an assumed 400 effective TFLOPS per GPU (Vega's token budget is not published): 100 million training tokens would take about **7 GPU-hours per member, roughly 29 for a four-member soup**, and a billion tokens about 290. Laya's 6-million-token demo comes to well under a GPU-minute on the same assumption. Hours to days on serious hardware: not free, but nothing like pretraining.

**LoRA's economics scale with the base model's size.** A 4B base needs about 8 GB for frozen weights and a 9B base about 16 GB, plus the adapter. A smaller base keeps training and serving cheap. A Laya-style encoder is the cheapest of all, at the price of its input and option limits.

## Part 4 — Calibration: can you trust the "90%"?

### 4.1 The weather forecaster

A forecaster says "70% chance of rain" on 100 days. A good forecaster gets rain on about 70 of them. A bad one says 70% and gets rain on 40. Both give a number, but only the first number *means* something.

A decision model is the same. When it says "billing, 90%", is it right about 90 times in 100? That property is **calibration**.

**Why you care.** The policies of Chapter 25, Part 5 (act above 0.9, ask a bigger model between 0.6 and 0.9, ask a person below 0.6; or act only above 62.5% when a wrong action costs three times a right one) all assume the percentages are honest. If a model says 95% and is right 60% of the time, an automatic rule lets a lot of mistakes through.

### 4.2 An experiment

A toy model was built to be over-confident, as many are: it exaggerates its certainty. It was given 4,000 test questions with 3 options each, and its answers were grouped by how sure it said it was:

```text
BEFORE calibration
  says 33-50% sure   → actually right 37% of the time
  says 50-70% sure   → right 47%
  says 70-90% sure   → right 54%
  says 90-100% sure  → right 71%      ← "I'm certain" was wrong 29 times in 100
```

On average the model claimed 74% confidence but was right only 54% of the time. That gap is the problem.

### 4.3 The fix: one dial

The raw scores go through a softmax, and a **temperature** divides them first. A bigger temperature flattens the answer:

```text
raw scores [2.1, -0.4, -1.3]
  temperature 1.0 → 90%, 7%, 3%      sharp
  temperature 1.5 → 77%, 15%, 8%     softer
```

Two things to notice:

- **The top choice never changes.** In the experiment all 4,000 answers were identical before and after. Only the *confidence* changes, so calibration makes the model **honest, not more accurate**: accuracy stayed at 54.4%.
- **The dial is found by trying values** on examples the model has not trained on, and picking the one that makes it least surprised by the true answers. That is cross-entropy again (Chapter 9). The search found 2.45, and the true value used to build the data was 2.5.

```text
AFTER calibration
  says 33-50% sure   → right 44%
  says 50-70% sure   → right 57%
  says 70-90% sure   → right 75%
  says 90-100% sure  → right 100%   (only 15 questions fell in this group)
```

The measure is **ECE** (expected calibration error): group the answers by stated confidence, find the gap between "what it said" and "how often it was right" in each group, and average the gaps, weighted by group size. It dropped from **0.197 to 0.016**.

### 4.4 What honest numbers buy you

Once the numbers mean what they say, you can choose how much to trust:

```text
answer only when confidence ≥ 0.5   → answers 61% of questions, 61% of them right
answer only when confidence ≥ 0.7   → answers 14% of questions, 76% of them right
```

That is a trade-off between **how many** questions you answer and **how often you are right**. (Without calibration, the same "90% or more" rule would have answered 24% of questions at only 71% right.) The toy data is deliberately noisy, so ignore the low overall accuracy: the point is the shape.

### 4.5 In Laya

All from its own documents and benchmark files:

- **Both published checkpoints ship over-confident.** The English one's ECE is **0.466** raw and **0.081** after temperature fitting (Jev's is reported as 0.246). The multilingual checkpoint ships with no fitted temperatures at all.
- **Its recipe:** hold out a calibration slice before training (up to 400 items or 10%), train, then fit one temperature per question type on that slice (a standard optimiser minimising the negative log-likelihood). The docs recommend going further, one per (question type, option count). The runtime clamps temperatures to 0.5 to 5.0.
- **Why the slice must be unseen:** on items it has already trained on, the model is near-certain and near-correct, so the fit has nothing to correct and returns a meaningless value.
- **Beyond temperature:** Laya's calibration module also fits **per-bucket abstention thresholds** (so that a gate keeps a target error rate in every bucket) and a **histogram-binning map** (a non-parametric correction for buckets whose reliability curve is not a simple softening). A **calibration file** (a small JSON holding the temperatures and any binning map, with no weights) can be installed on a loaded model.
- **Traps:** a stale `temperature_by_options` entry in a copied config silently overrides a new fit; for questions with 11 or more options the shipped bucket acted as a sharpener (a documented issue). There are also **two different "confidence" numbers**: `answer_confidence` is the top probability after temperature, while the plain `confidence` is entropy-based and sits on a different scale. Do not compare both to the same threshold.

### 4.6 Calibration's blind spot

Laya's own benchmark shows its English checkpoint scoring **0.0% accuracy at 95% confidence** on Khmer text. The model had stepped outside its world and was still sure of itself. Calibration fitted on in-distribution data does not catch this. That is why Laya routes by language **before** the model runs, and does not rely on confidence to notice.

## Part 5 — Keeping calibration alive

Models drift, because the world changes. Recalibration is the right first response to drift, because it is cheap: it changes a handful of numbers, not the network, and Laya stores calibration separately from the weights (Chapter 26, Part 2.8).

### 5.1 How many labelled examples before a refit?

On the toy model (true temperature 2.5), a temperature was fitted repeatedly on calibration sets of different sizes:

| Examples | Fitted temperature (typical error) |
|---|---|
| 20 | ±63% |
| 50 | ±35% |
| 100 | ±21% |
| **200** | **±16%** |
| 400 | ±9% |
| 1,000 | ±6% |

So **200 is workable for one temperature and 400 is comfortable**. A real model with more options needs more. Laya's own floors are: 10 examples for a per-type temperature (below that it stays at 1.0), 100 per bucket for abstention thresholds, 200 per bucket for a binning map, and 2,000 per bucket for per-option-count temperatures.

### 5.2 Two gates

Calibration fixes **honesty**; fine-tuning fixes **accuracy**. So a loop needs two gates:

```text
log (input, answer, scores, later outcome)
      │
      ▼
GATE 1, frequent and cheap:     at least 200 new labelled items AND the last 200 look dishonest
      │                          (ECE above a threshold)
      ▼                          refit the temperature → smooth → clamp → deploy the calibration file
GATE 2, rare and expensive:     accuracy over the last ~400 falls below your floor
      │                          EVEN AFTER recalibration (or errors cluster in one slice)
      ▼
queue a fine-tune: batch the corrected examples, train offline, validate, ship a new checkpoint
```

### 5.3 A simulation with drift

8,000 questions were streamed through a toy model, with the world made harder at question 3,000. Three policies: fit once and never again, refit every 200 labelled items, and refit only when the last 200 looked dishonest (ECE above 0.08).

**Scenario A: the world gets noisier.**

| Policy | Refits | ECE before drift | ECE, first 1,000 after | ECE, late | Accuracy before → late |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fit once | 1 | 0.023 | 0.118 | 0.086 | 55.9% → 47.3% |
| Refit every 200 | 40 | 0.024 | 0.048 | 0.011 | 55.9% → 47.3% |
| **Gated** | **17** | 0.017 | 0.051 | 0.011 | 55.9% → 47.3% |

**Scenario B** (the model's scores stop tracking reality) has the same shape: the static ECE rose after the drift, both live policies brought it back to about 0.013 to 0.016 (the gated one used 21 refits against 40), and accuracy fell from 55.9% to 52.8% under every policy.

What this shows:

1. Without recalibration, the confidence slowly became dishonest.
2. The gate matched refit-every-200 with roughly half the refits.
3. **Accuracy fell and no policy brought it back.** That drop is what should trigger the second gate and a fine-tune. Calibration restored honesty, not accuracy.

A gate costs little here, because refitting one number is cheap. It matters more when each refit needs real work, such as re-running the model over a buffer (Laya's helper re-runs the model on labelled examples to collect scores, so log the *inputs and outcomes*, not only the answers).

### 5.4 Smoothing and cautions

**Smoothing** (a suggestion, not simulated): take the median of the last three fits or a moving average of the logarithm of the temperature, clamp it, and only deploy a new value when it differs from the old by a margin (say 10%), so it does not flicker.

**Cautions.**

1. **Where do the labels come from?** If only low-confidence items get human review, you only learn the truth about those. Add a **random audit sample** of automatically handled items, or calibration will be biased.
2. **Labels arrive late** (a refund dispute, an outcome weeks later): calibrate on a rolling window of items whose outcome is known.
3. **Calibrate per slice** (language, workflow). One global temperature can hide a slice that is going wrong.
4. **Calibration will not catch out-of-world inputs** (Khmer): route those before the model.
5. **Keep a frozen test set and a rollback**, or recalibration can quietly mask a model that is getting worse. Laya's calibration file records which checkpoint it was fitted for and warns on a mismatch.

**What Laya has and lacks.** It has the pieces (the function that collects scores from labelled examples, the temperature, threshold and binning fitters, the calibration file, and an audit hook for logging requests). It has no automatic drift monitor or online updater that could be found, so the glue is yours.

## Part 6 — Evaluation: testing honestly

### 6.1 The driving-test idea

A student who practises on last year's exam paper and then sits *this* year's tells you about memory, not skill. A number only means something if you know what the model has seen, what it is compared with, who set the answers, and how much luck is in the result. Five questions, asked of any score:

**1. Did it train on this?** Laya's benchmark file marks every test "in training" or "held out" (Part 1.6). Spam scores 0.993 (in training); moderation, which the model has never seen, scores 0.530.

**2. Compared with what?** A number needs a reference point. On the typed-decisions test:

```text
random guessing                       0.318
always pick the common answer         0.461
Laya, base checkpoint                 0.362     ← below "always pick the common answer"
Laya, fine-tuned (author's figure)    0.766
Jev (published figure)                0.727
```

**3. Who decided the right answers?** Gold can itself be shaky. Typed-decisions gold is the average of three samples from a ~4B teacher (Part 1.4). One independent test's gold was one human reviewer correcting an AI draft. Another independent comparison used AI-assisted cases with no independent human annotation, and its 520 responses came from just 130 scenarios because variants of the same scenario are correlated.

**4. How much of it is luck?** Every accuracy number carries a margin of error. If each question were independent, the 95% margin on a 77% score is roughly:

| Questions | Margin |
|---|---|
| 100 | ±8.2 points |
| 400 | ±4.1 |
| 1,200 | ±2.4 |
| 2,000 | ±1.8 |

Two examples: **0.766 versus 0.727** on 2,000 decisions is a 3.9-point gap against a margin of about ±2.7 even if every question were independent (they are not: five questions share each case, so the true margin is wider), and the A/B test's **80.5% versus 79.1%** on 1,200 decisions is a 1.4-point gap against a margin of about ±3, within noise. A careful test resamples whole cases together (a clustered bootstrap), corrects for testing many suites, and **declines to claim** a gap that does not survive: one independent test refused to claim a 5.7-point Jev lead on triage (adjusted p = 0.063).

**5. Can any metric be gamed?** Yes. The typed-decisions card notes that a model that ignores the input and answers the commonest label has the **best ECE** (0.088) while knowing nothing, so read KL and Brier alongside ECE. Look at several measures together: accuracy, Brier or KL, ECE, **accuracy at each coverage level** (Part 4.4), and speed and cost.

### 6.2 Break it on purpose

| Stress test | What the sources show |
|---|---|
| **Reverse the option order** | On typed-decisions the base checkpoint changes **35.7%** of its choice answers when the options are reversed. Fine-tuning cuts that to about 6 to 8%. At 20 options: 18.7% base, falling to 1.9 to 2.6% with fine-tuning (and shuffling options during training). |
| **Many options** | Banking77 (77 labels): **0.425 against Jev's 0.870**, because the option text runs out of its token budget. |
| **A wrong "reviewer" comment** | One independent comparison added a deliberately misleading note to each case. **Laya's accuracy dropped to 19%.** |
| **Unfamiliar language** | The English checkpoint scored **0.0% at 95% confidence** on Khmer. |
| **Negation and labels** | The model card warns that `noul` can follow the wording of its option labels instead of the state. |
| **Long, noisy text** | A user report found near-chance accuracy on long multilingual posts. |

### 6.3 What the independent tests found

**Issue #555** (1,240 decisions across 9 suites; the default English Laya checkpoint, version 0.3.11, against Jev 1.13.0 and a small Qwen baseline):

| | Laya | Jev | Qwen2.5-1.5B baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| All suites | **0.686** | **0.907** | 0.605 |
| Triage | 0.875 | 0.932 | 0.781 |
| Moderation | 0.757 | 0.944 | 0.729 |
| 5-level score | 0.333 | 0.650 | 0.525 |

Jev led on all nine suites, and eight of the nine gaps held up after correction. Calibration was close for all three on choice questions. The test used the *English* checkpoint on every suite (including the multilingual one), and did not evaluate the fine-tuned checkpoint or the language router.

**A comparison of about 16 decision models** (Fabio Akita, 10 October 2026): on its compact set, Perplexity's 27B model scored 99.4%, Jev and Microsoft's Decision-1 97.6% each, a stock Qwen 4B with no decision training 74.4%, and a 144M model 31.5% (coin-flip level). Its verdict is that the factory Laya checkpoint is not suited to broad tasks, though it is among the few options that fit on weak devices; fine-tuned Laya was not tested.

**A fair reading.** These tests used the *base* English checkpoint zero-shot. That is consistent with Laya's own documentation, which says the base checkpoints are weak and that fine-tuning on your domain does most of the work. The honest summary: large decoder-based models and Jev were stronger out of the box, while Laya's strengths are size, speed, open weights, and what fine-tuning can do on your own data. Both tests have limits of their own (AI-assisted labels, correlated cases).

### 6.4 A checklist for testing a decision model

1. Collect **real examples from the target system**, 200 to 500 of them, with answers checked by a person.
2. Keep them **out of training**.
3. Report per question type and per slice (language, workflow), not only the average.
4. Compare against baselines: always the common answer, and a simple alternative such as embeddings plus similarity.
5. Report calibration **and** accuracy at several coverage levels.
6. Run the stress tests: reorder options, rename them, add distractors, add a misleading comment, try unfamiliar inputs.
7. Measure speed and cost.
8. Repeat the run, or use a clustered bootstrap, before believing a gap of a couple of points.

Laya ships a harness for much of this (`laya-evals`): scoring a labelled set, slice gates, abstention at a threshold, and comparing a run with a saved baseline as a build gate.

## Part 7 — Limits and open questions

- **Jev's internals are not public:** architecture, size, weights, training data and recipe. Everything in this chapter about *how* decision models work comes from the open ones.
- **Laya's original training scripts and data were not released** at launch (users asked for them in the first days); a training module and a public fine-tuning dataset have appeared since.
- **Vega's training data is undocumented** on its model card.
- **The act/escalate head does not work yet** in Laya's published checkpoints.
- **Independent evidence is thin.** Two independent tests exist, with the limits listed in Part 6, and none of them tested a Laya checkpoint fine-tuned on the test domain.
- **Order effects, many-option collapse, negation errors, unfamiliar languages and long noisy documents** are all documented weak spots (Part 6.2).
- **Calibration does not travel.** A temperature is right for the data it was fitted on, and needs refitting per setting (Part 5).
- **Priority and provenance claims** (who had the idea first) are claims, and the documents do not settle them.

## Common misconceptions, cleared up early

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| "LoRA makes training nearly free." | It saves **memory**, not compute, and Vega's adapter is 3.74 billion parameters. |
| "Fine-tuning is like training a GPT." | It is thousands to hundreds of thousands of times less compute than pretraining. |
| "RL is what makes Laya work." | A third-party test found no measurable gain over plain cross-entropy. Vega uses cross-entropy only. |
| "Calibration makes the model better at the task." | It makes the confidence honest. It never changes which answer is picked. |
| "Calibrate once and forget." | The right temperature drifts with the data. Use two gates: cheap recalibration, rare fine-tuning. |
| "A high benchmark score means it will work for me." | Only if the data was held out and resembles yours, and the gap exceeds the noise. |

```text
DATA      teacher probabilities (soft labels) teach honesty; hold out a calibration slice and a test set
TRAINING  cross-entropy on soft targets (+ ranked probability score for ordered levels); RL adds nothing measurable
COST      fine-tune ~10^16 FLOP vs pretraining 10^19-10^21; LoRA saves memory, not compute
CALIBRATE temperature fitted on held-out data: confidence honest, answers unchanged; refit via two gates
EVALUATE  held-out data · baselines · who made the gold · margin of error · stress tests · coverage curves
```
